What is real here and what is a stand-in

Real, from the plugin: the LaTeX compiler (now with z), the colour palettes and the saturating ramp, and the camera module that turns Desmos's three matrices into one. The arrows are drawn the way the 2D overlay draws them: the field is evaluated on the GPU, one draw call, no vertex buffer.

Stand-in: the graph underneath. It hands the overlay the same three matrices Desmos 3D reports, in the same format, but its field of view per perspective step is not Desmos's own. Desmos's real meshing and lighting are also different. Lining up with the real Desmos is part B, after this.

Checked against GPT's research round: it independently recommends shaded solid arrows that hide each other, stratified jitter rather than a lattice, a colour scale taken from a percentile of the field rather than from the box, and X-ray as the honest default. Its timings were guesses; the "Time 90 frames" button measures them on your GPU.

Arrows behind a surface: the options

Option How Cost and risk
(a) X-ray: draw over everything No occlusion. Depth comes from shading, fading, outlines and motion. Free, and never wrong about where an arrow is. An arrow behind a hill still shows in front of it.
(b) Hidden, our copy of the surface We redraw the surface you graphed, depth only, on our canvas, so it hides arrows behind it. Works for z = f(x, y) here. Implicit and parametric surfaces each need their own mesher. Where our mesh and Desmos's differ, arrows that touch the surface can flicker; the "Our surface vs the true one" figure measures that gap.
(b′) Faded, same copy As (b), but hidden parts are drawn faintly instead of removed. Same cost as (b). Suits translucent surfaces: try lowering the surface opacity.
(c) Desmos-native vectors Write vector(start, end) expressions and let Desmos depth-sort them. That is step 2. Shareable and always correct, but capped, and Desmos draws the arrowheads.
(d) Hook Desmos's renderer Draw inside Desmos's own WebGL context. Not shown here. Nothing has done this before: GLesmos, the obvious precedent, draws on its own canvas too and copies the picture into Desmos's 2D drawing. It would mean patching Desmos's private renderer, which breaks when Desmos ships a new build.
